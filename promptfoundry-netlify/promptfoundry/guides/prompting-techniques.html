Seven prompting techniques that hold up — and three that don't

Prompt advice spreads faster than prompt evidence. These seven techniques survived our test bench across six models. The last three are the popular ones we stopped using.

1. Few-shot examples

One or two examples of the output you want is still the highest-leverage addition to most prompts. Examples communicate length, register, structure and edge-case handling faster than any adjective.

  • Use it when: the output has a house style, a specific structure, or a tone that is hard to describe.
  • Watch out: the model will copy your example's content, not just its shape. Pick examples whose subject matter differs from the task.

2. Decomposition

Asking for the plan first and the artefact second beats asking for both at once. "List the sections, wait for my approval, then write section one" produces better long documents than "write a 2,000-word report", because it stops the model from committing to a structure before it has thought about one.

3. Scratchpad before answer

On reasoning-heavy tasks, a scratchpad — a place to think before the deliverable — measurably reduces confident errors. With Claude, XML tags like <scratchpad> work; with other models, "think through the trade-offs in three bullets, then give your recommendation" is enough. The point is separation: reasoning in one place, artefact in another, so the artefact stays clean.

4. Explicit output schemas

If the output feeds a spreadsheet, a database or another model, define the schema. Name the keys, the types and what to return when a value is unknown. Vague JSON is worse than prose: it fails silently downstream. "Return valid JSON with keys deliverable, assumptions and next_steps; use null for unknown values" is the minimum.

5. Negative constraints

Models are better at avoiding specific, checkable things than at achieving vague qualities. "No exclamation marks. Never open with 'I hope this finds you well'. Ban the words revolutionary and seamless." Each ban is testable, which means you can tell whether it worked — and reject the output when it didn't. Bans also survive model upgrades better than adjectives, because they describe observable behaviour.

6. Self-check instructions

One line — "before you answer, check the output against every constraint and fix it silently" — is the cheapest quality gain available. A stronger version tells the model what to do with uncertainty: "if a required input is missing, ask up to three questions instead of inventing an answer." This is the difference between a confident wrong answer and a useful pause.

7. Role framing with judgement, not adjectives

Roles work when they describe priorities and refusals, not status. "You are a staff engineer who blocks on security and can live with ugly code" outperforms "you are a 10x expert engineer" because it tells the model which trade-off to make when two constraints collide. Every real task has that collision; most prompts leave it unresolved.

Three popular techniques that underperformed

"Act as a world-class expert" (no specifics)

In our runs, generic status-pumping changed tone more than quality. What moved output quality was specificity about the audience and the judgement to apply. The magic phrase is not magic; the missing context is the problem.

Asking for "10x" or "best possible" output

Superlatives give the model no lever. "Best possible" cannot be checked, so it cannot be met. Replace the superlative with a rubric: "every bullet must contain a number, a named party and a next action" is a quality bar. "Make it world-class" is a mood.

Longer and longer prompts

Length is not rigour. Past a point, extra paragraphs dilute the instructions that matter. We cut prompts that fail into their essential constraints first. If a prompt is over 400 words, ask what you could delete without changing the behaviour — and delete it. Keep the rest for edge cases as a separate prompt.

How to know if a technique actually worked

Run it ten times, score the outputs against a fixed rubric, and keep the results next to the prompt. Without that, you are remembering your best sample, not measuring quality. Our testing guide has the exact protocol, and the lab page shows what it looks like in practice.
